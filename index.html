A Chinese invention, over about a thousand years

The South-Pointing Needle

The first Chinese compass pointed south, and it was used to place buildings and graves long before anyone steered a ship by it.

From a spoon to a needle

No single person invented the compass. It grew in steps, and historians still disagree about some of them. Each date below comes from the source named beside it.

  1. 278 to 209 BCEDiviners' boards

    Boards called shi were buried in tombs from this period. They are the oldest precursors of the markings later used on compasses, and the markings stayed virtually unchanged from the boards to the first magnetic compasses. (Wikipedia, Luopan)

  2. 2nd century BCE to 2nd century CEThe south-pointer

    During the Han dynasty, a spoon-shaped piece of lodestone called the sinan ("south-governor") appears in records. It was used for geomancy and fortune-telling, and possibly to orient buildings and choose house sites. (Wikipedia, History of the compass) (Smith College)

  3. About 70 to 80 CEWang Chong writes it down

    In the Lunheng, Wang Chong records that a south-pointing spoon thrown on the ground comes to rest pointing south. One source dates the book to around 60 CE. (Wikipedia, History of the compass) (Hakai Magazine)

  4. 7th and 8th centuriesNeedles

    By the Tang dynasty, scholars magnetised iron needles by rubbing them with magnetite. A needle could float in water, balance on a pointed shaft or hang from a silk thread. (Smith College)

  5. 1040 to 1044The iron fish

    The military manual Wujing Zongyao describes a heated iron fish floating in water to find south on dark nights or in gloomy weather. Zeng Gongliang was chief editor. (Wikipedia, Wujing Zongyao) (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

  6. 1088Shen Kuo's needle

    Shen Kuo's Dream Pool Essays describes magnetising a steel needle with lodestone and favours hanging it on a silk thread. (Wikipedia, Shen Kuo) (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

  7. About 1117At sea

    Zhu Yu's Pingzhou Ketan says ship pilots used stars and the sun in clear weather and the south-pointing needle in dark weather. One source dates the book to 1111 to 1117, and another gives 1119 for the first reports of a compass used this way. (Wikipedia, History of the compass) (Columbia, Asia for Educators)

  8. About 1190Europe writes about it

    Alexander Neckam gives the earliest European description of a magnetised needle used by sailors. (Wikipedia, Alexander Neckam) (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

Spoon on a plate

First for feng shui, then for ships

The earliest uses were about place and fortune, not travel. Geomancy came long before navigation. (Smith College)

The heaven plate

The Han spoon sat on a cast bronze plate marked with the eight trigrams of the I Ching, 24 directions and 28 lunar mansions. The Big Dipper was often drawn in the centre. The square stood for earth and the circle for heaven. (Smith College)

Finding good places

One use was finding the best location and time for burials. Early compasses may also have been used to orient buildings and choose house sites, crop areas and gem-searching locations. (Wikipedia, History of the compass) (Smith College)

The luopan

A feng shui practitioner uses a luopan to find the exact direction of a structure or place. It can have up to 40 concentric rings, and a typical one marks 24 directions of 15 degrees each. (Wikipedia, Luopan)

Which way it points

A luopan's needle points to the south magnetic pole, while a typical modern compass points to the north magnetic pole. (Wikipedia, Luopan)

Taking it to sea

Better needles made the compass useful away from land.

A smaller, steadier needle

In Song times the needle was made smaller and fixed on a stem instead of floating in water. Sometimes it sat in a small case with a glass top, which suited sea travel. (Columbia, Asia for Educators)

Shen Kuo and true north

Shen Kuo wrote that needles are always displaced slightly east rather than pointing due south. He also noted that a needle sometimes pointed south and sometimes north. (Wikipedia, Shen Kuo)

Dark weather

Zhu Yu records that pilots used the stars and sun when it was clear and the south-pointing needle when it was dark. A navigation map from Zheng He's voyages, the Mao Kun Map, contains "needle records". (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

Not everyone agrees on when

One estimate puts the Chinese maritime compass at some time before 1050, possibly as early as 850. Clear written evidence of use at sea appears only in 1117. The historian Kreutz argued that Chinese sailors used only floating needles in bowls until the 16th century, and that steering by compass did not really begin until 1410. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

How it reached Europe and the Islamic world

Written records appear in these places at these times. How the compass travelled between them is uncertain. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

About 1190Alexander Neckam describes a needle used by sailors. He probably heard in Paris that ships carried a magnetised needle on a pivot. (Wikipedia, Alexander Neckam)
1232The earliest Islamic-world reference, in the Persian talebook Jawami ul-Hikayat. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)
1269Petrus Peregrinus describes a dry compass for seafaring. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)
1282The earliest Arabic reference, by Baylak al-Qibjaqi. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

How did it get there? Some historians think Arabs introduced it to Europe. Others propose transmission across the Indian Ocean or through the Crusades, and some propose that Europeans invented it themselves. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

What we do not know

Was the spoon a compass?

Wang Chong's passage does not mention magnetism. The idea that the spoon was the earliest magnetic compass is widely held, but it rests on his written description. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

Does a Han spoon survive?

The Hakai article describes no surviving Han-era spoon and no test of one. Its only working example is a 1999 replica made from sheet steel by a Smith College student, which has since lost its magnetism. (Hakai Magazine)

Was the compass only Chinese?

The compass is credited to China, but its development is described as "highly uncertain". The history page also notes some scholars propose independent invention in Europe. (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

Did the iron fish last?

The iron fish was magnetised by heating, which gave only weak magnetisation. After Shen Kuo's magnetised needle, this thermoremanence compass fell out of use. (Wikipedia, Wujing Zongyao) (Wikipedia, History of the compass)

Terms

Sinan
"South-governor" or "south-pointer", the Han name for the lodestone spoon.
Lodestone
A naturally magnetic rock, a form of magnetite ore.
Thermoremanence
Magnetism gained by heating iron and letting it cool in line with the Earth's field. It is weak.
Declination
The angle between where a compass needle points and true north or south.
Geomancy
Choosing sites and times by reading the land and the heavens. Feng shui is one form.
Luopan
The feng shui compass, with a rotating dial and many rings of formulas.
Heaven plate
The bronze board the Han spoon sat on, marked with directions and star groups.
Dry compass
A compass whose needle sits on a pivot instead of floating in water.
